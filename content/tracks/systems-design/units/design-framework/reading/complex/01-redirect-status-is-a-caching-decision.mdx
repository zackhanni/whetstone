title: 301 vs 302 is a caching decision
---

The rule is in the [HTTP status codes](/learn/reference-cards/http-status-codes) reference cards: RFC 9110 makes 301 and 308
**heuristically cacheable**, and 302 and 307 not. "Heuristically cacheable" means a browser or
shared cache may store the redirect and reuse it **without asking you again**, even though you
sent no `Cache-Control` (RFC 9111 lets a cache store a response that carries no caching headers
at all only when its status code is defined as heuristically cacheable). A 302 or 307 is stored
only if its headers allow it: a lifetime (`max-age`, `s-maxage`, `Expires`), or an explicit
`Cache-Control: public` — which, with no lifetime, makes even a 302 heuristically cacheable
(RFC 9111 §5.2.2.9). So check the headers your redirect actually goes out with, not just its
status.

For a shortener, that rule becomes two product consequences:

- **Clicks disappear.** A returning visitor's browser follows its stored 301 and never reaches
  your server, so the click count undercounts — silently, and most for your most loyal users.
- **Destinations stop being yours to change.** If a link is edited or taken down after a
  phishing report, clients holding a cached 301 keep going to the old target until their cache
  lets it go. You can purge a CDN; you can't purge a stranger's browser.

A 302 (or 307) with none of those headers isn't stored, so every click comes back through you.
That's the trade: **you serve every redirect** — exactly the load the estimates planned for — in
exchange for **counting and controlling every redirect**. A shortener that never edits links and
doesn't sell analytics could choose 301 and let browsers absorb repeat traffic. That's a
legitimate answer too, as long as you say what it costs.

Explicit headers override the defaults in both directions: a 302 with
`Cache-Control: max-age=…` (or just `public`) becomes storable, and a 301 with
`Cache-Control: no-store` must not be stored. Which directive does what, and how a CDN reads `s-maxage`, is the Next.js track's
[Response headers: HTTP caching & compression](/learn/nextjs-and-the-network/response-headers-caching-compression) unit. The design-level point to say out loud: **the status
code is a decision about who may cache, so make it from the requirements** (analytics,
editability, takedowns) — not from whether "permanent" sounds right.

<!--more-->

Those overrides give you a dial instead of a switch. A 302 with
`Cache-Control: private, max-age=300` lets each browser absorb its own user's repeat clicks for
five minutes while keeping shared caches out. A takedown then reaches every browser within five
minutes, and the count misses only repeats inside that window. "How fast must a takedown take
effect?" has become a number in a header — and the number comes from the requirements.
