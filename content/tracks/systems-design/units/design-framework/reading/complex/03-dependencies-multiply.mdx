id: dependencies-multiply
title: Availability multiplies across hard dependencies
---

The 99.9% target is for the whole redirect, and the redirect has parts. AWS's reliability
pillar states the rule: when a failure in a dependency is a failure for you — a **hard
dependency** — your availability is the **product** of the availabilities involved, your own
included.

```
redirect service 99.9%  ×  database 99.9%  ×  cache 99.9%
  = 0.999³ ≈ 0.997  →  99.7%, about 26 hours of downtime a year instead of 8¾
```

The product assumes the parts fail **independently** — AWS's worked example says "independent
systems". Outages that overlap (one bad deploy taking down all three at once) leave the path
better off, up to 99.9%; outages that never overlap leave it at 1 − 3 × 0.1% = 99.7%. Until you
know which, about 99.7% is the number you can promise.

The same page names two ways back up:

- **Redundancy adds nines.** Two independent copies, each 99.9% available, are down together
  only 0.1% × 0.1% of the time — 99.9999% for that component. The word doing the work is
  *independent*: the same region, the same deploy or the same bad config is how two copies fail
  together.
- **Make it a soft dependency** — one whose failure your application compensates for. If the
  cache is down, read the database instead, and a cache outage costs latency rather than
  availability.

The second fix hides a trap that the AWS Builders' Library's caching article describes: once a
cache has worked for a while, the database behind it gets scaled down to the load the cache lets
through, so a cold or failed cache sends a surge downstream. Put your own number on it. At a 95%
hit ratio (an assumption), the database normally sees the 5% of reads that miss; with the cache
gone it sees all of them — **20× its usual read load**. The "soft" dependency just moved the
outage one layer down. The fallback needs a cap (shed load, keep a small in-process cache, serve
fewer requests well), and the article's advice is to run load tests with caches disabled.
"We fall back to the database" is a claim to test, not a design. (What each Redis-backed piece
does when Redis is unreachable is the [Caching & rate limits with Redis](/learn/backend-nestjs/nest-redis-caching-and-rate-limits) unit's complex tier.)

<!--more-->

Recovery time is the other lever. The same AWS page estimates availability as
MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR) — mean time between failures over itself plus mean time to recover. A
failure every 150 days that takes an hour to fix is 3,600 ÷ 3,601 hours ≈ 99.97%. Unavailability
is roughly MTTR ÷ MTBF, so halving the recovery time (fast rollback, automated failover) cuts
downtime about as much as making failures half as frequent — and is often the cheaper of the two
to build.
