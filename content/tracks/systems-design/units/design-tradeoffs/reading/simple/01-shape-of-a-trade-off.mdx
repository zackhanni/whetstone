title: The four parts of a trade-off argument
---

"It depends" is where a design answer starts, not where it ends. A trade-off argument a reviewer
can actually check has four parts:

1. **The options** — at least two real ones, named concretely.
2. **The deciding fact** — the requirement or number that tips it. Not "scale"; *which* number.
3. **What you give up** — the cost of the option you pick, said out loud before anyone asks.
4. **What would change your mind** — the measurement or event at which you'd switch.

A small example — how long should a cached product page live?

> **Options:** a 5-minute TTL, or delete-on-write plus a 1-hour TTL as a safety net.
> **Deciding fact:** prices change a few times a day, and the requirement says a shopper may see
> a price up to a minute old — but never pay a different price than the one shown, which checkout
> re-reads from the database anyway.
> **Give up:** delete-on-write means every price update has to know about the cache — one more
> thing a new write path can forget.
> **Change my mind:** if a second service starts writing prices without going through our code,
> deletes become unreliable and I'd drop to a short TTL alone.

Notice what isn't in it: "Redis is fast", "best practice", "it scales". Each line is about *this*
system's numbers and requirements, which is what makes it possible to disagree with one line
instead of the whole answer.

Part 4 is the one most often skipped, and it's the most useful. It turns a decision into a
monitor: "if the hit ratio drops below 90%", "if one account passes 100k followers", "if p99
exceeds 50 ms". A design that names its own reversal point can be revisited calmly instead of in
an incident.

<!--more-->

The same four parts work when the honest answer is "not yet". *Options:* add read replicas now,
or wait. *Deciding fact:* the primary runs at 20% CPU at peak. *Give up:* headroom bought before
it's needed. *Change my mind:* sustained peak CPU over 60%, or a launch that the estimates say
triples reads. Deferring with a trigger is a decision; deferring without one is drift.
