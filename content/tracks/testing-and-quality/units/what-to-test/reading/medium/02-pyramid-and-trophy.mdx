title: Pyramid, trophy, and where one check belongs
---

Every test sits somewhere on a trade-off:

- **Low-level tests** (a function on its own) are fast, cheap to write, and point at the exact line
  that broke. But they replace the rest of the system, so they can't tell you the pieces work
  together.
- **High-level tests** (a real browser driving the deployed app) give the most confidence that
  users are fine. They're slow, need the whole stack running, and fail for reasons unrelated to
  your change — a slow network, a shared test account.

web.dev's testing-strategy guide puts it as: *"the higher up you climb the pyramid, the more
confident you get"*, and confidence *"is in direct competition with the resources you need to
execute the tests."* The common diagrams are two answers to "so where do most tests go?"

| Shape | Credited to | The bulk of the tests | Top |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Test pyramid** | Mike Cohn, *Succeeding with Agile* | unit tests | few end-to-end |
| **Testing trophy** | Kent C. Dodds | integration tests, on a base of static analysis (types, lint) | few end-to-end |

They agree on more than they disagree: end-to-end tests are the most expensive, so you keep few;
lower levels carry most of the load. The trophy moves the bulk up one level, to tests where several
real modules work together, and counts static analysis as the base layer — TypeScript and a linter
catch a class of bugs before any test runs. web.dev's own conclusion is *"It depends"* — on your
app, not on a diagram.

## Placing a single check

Diagrams describe a whole suite. For one behaviour, ask: **what's the lowest level at which this
can actually break?**

- The discount rounds half-cents correctly → a unit test of the pure function. Nothing else can
  make rounding wrong.
- The checkout handler passes the right cart to the pricing module and saves the result → an
  integration test with both real modules and a test database.
- The "Pay" button is hidden behind a cookie banner on small screens → only a real browser can
  see that. That's an end-to-end test.

Testing a rounding rule end-to-end works, but it costs a browser and a running backend per case and
can't easily reach the half-cent inputs. Testing the button with a unit test is impossible — the
bug isn't in any one unit.
